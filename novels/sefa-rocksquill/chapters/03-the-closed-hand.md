# Chapter Three — The Closed Hand

She woke to the smell of clean wax and crushed feverbalm, which was a better smell than the one she had gone to sleep in. White light. A high domed room of pale bone, scrubbed and lime-washed, tall slot-windows letting the morning gales sing through gauze. She was face-down on a padded surgeon's cradle, arms strapped loose to the rests, and her ruined left wing was spread out to the side on a board, pinned open under a frame of little hooks so it could not move while it was worked. She could not feel it. There was a numbing draught in her, her tongue thick with it, and a warm distant tugging where she knew the pain ought to be.

A voice above and behind her shoulder, dry and unhurried. "Awake. Good. I prefer them awake — a wing tells me more when its owner can flinch."

The surgeon came round into sight as she circled the board: an old Roak woman, broad and stooped, hands gloved in waxed kidskin already pink at the fingertips with Sefa's blood, a jeweller's loupe strapped over one eye. Her apron was Featherwright grey. And at her throat, on a plain cord, was a small worked-bone pin — a closed hand. The same craft, the same mark Sefa had last seen on Vesh, two breaths before she took his wing off and the room went up.

The surgeon followed her eyes to it without looking up from her work. "You're staring at my pin. They all do, this week. Word travels." A small, careful tug at something deep in the wing-root; the warmth shifted. "So let's have it plain before I've got your flight-tendon in my fingers, because I don't work over a patient who thinks I'm the enemy. Yes. I am Featherwright. Yes, I knew Vesh — I trained the hands that burned in your fire. And yes, I took the Fallen Fund's coin to put a Warden's wing back together this morning." The loupe-eye swivelled to fix her, huge and wet. "Now. Ask me what you came up wanting to ask. I'll answer with my mouth while my hands keep working. I find that's the honest way."

Face-down, strapped, drooling numbing-draught, Sefa took the only shot she had.

"Ser. I'll be honest with you. You're the second Featherwright wing-surgeon I've been this close to in two days." A beat. "The first one I had to take apart. So forgive me if my standards have come down. You've already got me beat — you've had your hands in my back for an hour and you haven't once tried to set the room on fire."

The tugging in her wing paused. Then the old woman made a sound Sefa didn't expect — a short, dry bark of a laugh, gravel rolled in a cup. "Hah. 'Standards come down.' Good. They should." A hook clicked. "And you're not wrong to keep score. But you can stop watching my pin like it's going to bite. If I wanted you dead I'd not be sweating over your flight-tendon on a Fund chit that doesn't cover my candle-oil. I'd have closed you crooked and let the air do the rest." She said it as mildly as weather. "The name's Brede. Wing-Master Ossla Brede."

"What was he like," Sefa said. "Vesh. Any thought on why he went to the stillness."

Brede threaded something fine through the wing-root — Sefa felt the whisper of it, a thin bright wire that made her good wing twitch against its strap — and tied it off before she spoke.

"Gentle. That's the thing nobody believes after, but he was. Quiet hands, quiet voice, remembered the names of every patient's children. The apprentices loved him. *I* loved him, the way you love the best thing you ever made." A clamp clicked. "And underneath it, nothing. No floor. Most of us patch a wing because we can't stand to watch a creature grounded — it hurts us, so we fix it. Vesh fixed wings the way you'd square a crooked shelf. Tidy. Correct. He never felt the patient at all. Then a moult-sick fledge came in — wings come in wrong, a year of hard surgery ahead of her and maybe flight at the end, maybe not — and Vesh looked at all that striving and decided the kind thing, the clean thing, was to stop it. Stop her."

The loupe-eye came up. "That's your answer, if you want my thought. The Choir didn't turn him. The Choir gave him *words* for what was already sitting where his heart should have been. You've heard them preach — the world is sick with trying, the gales and the flying and the clawing to stay aloft all suffering, and the Stilling is the world being allowed to rest. Set down the wing. To a man who never once felt the joy of the thing, who only saw flight as a problem he was paid to correct, that's not madness. It's the first idea that ever made him feel like he was right." She made a small contemptuous noise. "Far worse than madness. A madman you can pity. Vesh was *comforted*."

She straightened, pressed the small of her own stooped back. "You want to know what frightens me. It's a good sermon. I've buried a husband and three apprentices to bad seasons, and on the worst nights I can hear why someone tired enough would want to walk into the quiet and stop. Vesh wasn't a monster who tricked the desperate. He was a desperate man who believed the comfort. There'll be more of him. There's always more tired people than there are reasons to keep flapping."

---

"Hell," Sefa said into the padding. "I'll take a little discomfort and reality over comfort and muffled silence every day. Is that all it is, though? Get people to lie down in a soft bed, and that's all you need to make them watch the ones they love burn? Fuck that." A beat. "...Pardon me. Sorry."

Brede snorted. "I've had my hands inside Wardens for forty years, girl, I've heard every word there is and invented a few." She waved the curse off with two bloody fingers. But the rest of it landed somewhere and stayed. "'Discomfort and reality over comfort and silence.' Aye. That's the whole argument against the Choir in a mouthful, and it took a kestrel face-down on my table to say it plain." Quieter, then, the thing under the thing. "You're right to be sick over how cheap it is. A soft bed and a kind voice and a name for your tiredness. That's all the closed hand needs."

*The closed hand.* She had not said *Vesh* that time. She had said the guild.

So Sefa went for it — not as a Warden working an asset, but straight. *This matters. You're already on the right side of it. Help us.* Brede's hands slowed. Her face closed a notch — not hostile, careful, the look of a woman doing sums she didn't like. Because what was really being asked was for a Featherwright to inform on Featherwrights, and she had forty years and a guild-pension riding on that grey apron.

"...You're not just making conversation," she said. "You're recruiting me."

For a long moment the only sound was the gulls and the gauze. Then she set down her hook, peeled the loupe off her eye, and looked at Sefa properly for the first time. "Forty years I've kept my head down and my hands clean. Patched whoever the Fund sent, asked no questions, took no sides. And I'm too old and too tired to watch the thing I gave my life to turn into a machine for talking sad people into dying." She exhaled, long. "So. Yes. On my terms — I'm no one's bird on a string, and I'll not be skulking or wearing a wire to guild-hall. But I'll talk to your commander. The real one, Veil-Torn. And I'll tell her what I'll tell you now, as earnest of it."

She tapped the closed hand at her throat. "This isn't guild issue. Most Featherwrights never see one. It marks an old fellowship inside the guild — the Stillhand. Hundreds of years back it was a *kindness*: the surgeons who sat with the dying, who'd ease a hopeless case out clean when the alternative was a month of screaming. Mercy that meant what the word means. I took my pin at thirty, and I've eased more good deaths than I can count, and I'll not apologise for one." Her jaw tightened. "But the Choir's been whispering in the Stillhand's ear a long while. Some of the younger ones don't say 'ease the dying' anymore. They say 'ease the suffering,' and they've decided everyone's suffering, and the whole world's a hopeless case. Vesh wore this pin. So do four or five others I could name who'd have called what he did beautiful. And so do I — because somebody who remembers what it meant needs to still be in the room when they vote."

Sefa couldn't reach her sword — it was somewhere across the room and her arms were strapped — so she did the only thing she could, which was say it, and mean it.

"Wing-Master. What you just did took more nerve than anything I did in that fire." She waited until the loupe-eye lifted. "You've got a Warden's word. Mine. Whatever the Stillhand is when this is over — whoever doesn't like that you opened the door — they come through me first. I can't lift a wing right now, but I can still lift a wingspear. My sword's yours, ser. That's not Warden manners. That's a debt."

Brede held her eye a long beat, and then she did the gruff thing, because surgeons don't know how to be thanked. "Hmf. A grounded kestrel and a tired old butcher. The Stillhand must be quaking." But her voice had gone thick, and she busied her hands with a clamp that didn't need adjusting, and only half-managed the joke before she muttered, almost too low to catch, "...It's been a while since anyone offered to stand between me and anything."

Then she was back to business, where she was safe. "Now. You'll not fly for a week, and you'll not trust that wing in a real gale for two. Push it before then and you'll undo my morning and your own sky both. So whatever you're scheming, you do it sitting down."

Which solved the problem, when Sefa thought about it. Brede couldn't be seen walking into a Warden hall — but there was nothing in the world more ordinary than a company-commander coming to a quiet surgery to sit at the bedside of the Warden who'd burned a wing off saving six families. The meet didn't need a back alley. It needed Esk to visit her wounded. Cover that was true.

---

The fastest, most trustworthy pair of wings she knew was almost certainly downstairs, pacing a hole in the landing and refusing to go home until someone told her Sefa had woken. Brede cracked the door and barked it down the stair-throat, and there were boots before she'd finished.

Kithrel came through fast, soot still in the seams of her, eyes going to the pinned-open wing and back to Sefa's face. "There she is." The relief cracked her voice; she covered it with a scowl, shut the door with her heel, dropped low. "What. What's happened."

So Sefa told her. All of it, not coy — Vesh and what he was, the closed-hand pin, the Stillhand gone rotten, Brede turned to their side, the names she held, the plan: Esk comes here, to visit her wounded Warden, and the meet wears that for a coat. Kithrel listened with her jaw getting harder, glanced once at Brede, who lifted two bloody fingers in a dry salute, and breathed out slow. "Saints. A night off and you start a war with a guild."

Then, lower, while she leaned in like she was just fussing the blanket: *Get me a knife. Something small. Where I can reach it.* Her hands didn't pause — she'd smuggled worse than steel — and a moment later there was a cold familiar weight tucked along Sefa's good forearm under the blanket, hilt to her fingers. "For a surgery full of healers," Kithrel murmured, "you're awfully sure someone in here wants you opened the wrong way."

Which was exactly the thought Sefa couldn't put down. Brede had named *four or five others.* And a Featherwright surgery was full of Featherwright hands. So while Kithrel straightened, she stopped bantering and *looked* — the survey-flier's slow sweep, reading the room for the one thing that didn't sit right.

The brazier-boy: nothing. The hand moving gauze in the next room: a wedding-tune, oblivious. Brede: Brede. And then the apprentice at the long instrument-table. Vael, young, maybe seventeen, narrow shoulders — and she had been laying out the same tray of hooks for the length of the whole conversation. Set them, squared them, lifted them, set them again. Angled wrong, too: not at her table but a quarter-turn toward the cradle, the ear forward. And when Sefa had said the word *Stillhand*, the girl's hands had stopped. Just for a breath. Then started again, too smooth. She turned to rack a clamp and her collar gapped, and there it was — a small worked-bone glint on a cord at her throat. A closed hand.

But the look gave Sefa the thing worth the looking: the girl wasn't coiled. She was *terrified.* White at the knuckle, breath too shallow, eyes fixed hard on her tray so she wouldn't find Sefa watching. Not an assassin. A frightened kid who'd come in to tidy a wing-surgery and overheard her whole secret fellowship being sold to the Wardens — and who, the second she found a reason to step out, was going to run, straight to whoever held her leash.

She was four metres off. Sefa was flat on a board and couldn't cross them. The girl's eyes flicked, once, to the door.

So Sefa took her name off Brede's lips — she caught the old surgeon's eye, and Brede read her in a heartbeat and breathed a single word, *"Pell"* — and called to her, soft and firm, the voice for a spooked flier on a ledge.

"Pell. Don't. Don't go for that door yet. Look at me." Low and even. "I'm strapped to a board with my wing in pieces and the Wing-Master needs a second pair of steady hands, and yours are steadier than mine right now by a long way. So come here. Hold the lamp for her. That's all I'm asking. Just — come hold the lamp."

Not *I see your pin.* Not *don't you dare warn them.* A wounded woman asking a scared kid for help. A reason to step toward the room instead of out of it.

For one terrible breath the girl was frozen between the door and the table — and then the thing Sefa had gambled on won. A frightened person handed a small honest task instead of an accusation takes the task like a rope thrown in water. Pell's shoulders dropped. She set the tray down — carefully, even now — and crossed the four metres toward the cradle, and lifted the lamp in both shaking hands and held it steady over the opened wing. Close now. Inside reach. The pin plain at her open collar, rising and falling too fast, a tear hung on her lower lashes she was too scared to blink loose.

"...Steady, girl," Brede said, softer than Sefa had heard her. "Bit higher with the lamp. Good. That's good." Giving the kid a place to put her hands.

Sefa let the lamp settle her, and then spoke under the brazier-crackle, just for the two of them.

"I'm not going to call the guard. I'm not going to take your pin, and Kithrel by the door isn't going to touch you. You heard everything I said about Vesh and the Stilling and what that fire was, so you already know I'm not angry at a frightened girl holding a lamp." She let it sit. "I think you're scared of the right things, Pell. I think you took that pin believing it meant the kind thing — sitting with the dying, easing the hopeless — the way it meant for the Wing-Master here for forty years. And somewhere lately you started hearing them say it about people who *aren't* dying. And it frightened you. That's not weakness. That's your conscience working exactly the way it's meant to." The girl's knuckles whitened on the lamp; the tear fell. "So I'll ask you one thing, and you can say no, and you'll still walk out of here on your own feet. Who has your leash, Pell? Who do you report to?"

The name was right there — the girl's lips parted, a syllable forming, her whole self leaning across the lamplight —

— and Sefa, every nerve bent on reaching her, leaned in too hard. The blanket slid off her forearm. And the lamplight caught the steel.

The hidden knife. Hilt-to-hand along her wrist, bared in the full light right under the girl's eyes.

She watched it happen in Pell's face and there was nothing she could do to stop it: every gentle thing she'd said inverted. Not a wounded woman asking for help — a strapped-down Warden who knew her name, knew about the pin, had a blade hidden the whole time, and was *working her.* The lamp, the kindness, *I'm not going to take your pin* — all of it, a trap with teeth. The girl's heart broke the wrong way.

"You—" Her voice sheared into something thin and terrified. She didn't name her handler. She didn't bolt clean. She just came apart, and the lamp left her hands.

It tumbled, flame guttering, straight down toward the tray of spirit-soaked swabs and the edge of the board where the wing lay open.

"*Kithrel—!*"

Sefa didn't get a word past her name. Kithrel had been a courier before she was a Warden, and a thing falling was the oldest reflex she owned — two strides, and she caught the lamp a handspan above the swabs, folded it into her body, smothered the wick against her glove. A curl of smoke, the stink of singed leather, and no fire. The board, the wing, the swabs: untouched.

The other half — the words for Pell — those weren't there. Sefa reached for the one true thing that might have caught her and her tongue found nothing, and it didn't matter, because the girl was long past hearing. She saw the knife. There is no sentence in the world that un-sees a hidden blade. She didn't run and she didn't scream — the sound climbing her throat never made it out. She folded. Her legs went, and she slid down the wall to the floor, knees to her chest, both hands over her face, a thin broken keening leaking between her fingers. Undone by her own terror more thoroughly than anything Sefa could have done to her. Contained. The fire out. The surgery quiet but for the sobbing.

And utterly not hers. No name. No turn.

Brede had stopped working. She was staring at the heap of her apprentice, her old face gone grey. "...Pell," she said, very quietly. "Oh, child."

Sefa made the call that cost something. She stood down. First the knife — she eased it back out of sight beneath the blanket, slow and obvious, *let her see it disappear,* because the worst thing in this room was a blade and a Warden's hard face, and she could subtract one of those. Then she went still. She caught Kithrel's eye and tipped her head at the door — *quiet, watch it, let the girl be* — and gave Brede the floor, because Brede was the only one who could stand on it. "Wing-Master. She's yours. I'll keep still and keep my mouth shut."

Brede laid a wax-paper swab over the open wing-root, stripped a glove with her teeth, and crossed to the floor. What knelt down by Pell was just an old woman who'd buried too many people. She didn't reach for the pin. She didn't say *traitor.* She got down to the girl's level, both knees on the cold bone, and waited until the sobbing hitched enough that words might fit.

"Pell. Look at my collar. Go on. You took yours for the same reason I took mine. The sitting-up with the dying. The hand held at the end so no one goes into the dark alone. That's what it meant, child. That's what you swore. And somewhere this winter they started asking you to swear to something else, and it's been eating you hollow, and you didn't have anyone to say it to." Her voice cracked. "You do now. I never left. Talk to me."

For a moment it seemed to work. The sobbing eased; Pell's hands came down a little; she looked at the closed hand at Brede's throat and up at the wet, earnest eyes, and Sefa saw her *want* to fall into it, want to be the apprentice again.

And then something in her face closed like a shutter.

"...You too," she whispered. Almost too quiet to hear. "They got you too." She didn't mean the Wardens. She meant the world — the great betrayal the Choir promised was everywhere, the proof that everyone who walks out of the quiet is lost to the noise and the cruelty of it. Her own master, kneeling in a Warden's surgery beside a Warden's blade-girl — to Pell, in that moment, that was not rescue. It was the last person she trusted proving the sermon right. She pulled her knees back to her chest and went quiet — not calm, closed, wrapped around the pin and the doctrine like they were the only warm thing left.

Brede stayed down a moment longer, a hand hovering near the girl's shoulder, not quite landing. Then she got up, slowly, and the look she turned on Sefa was the most defeated thing in the room. "...I can set a wing," she said, low and bitter. "Forty years, I can set any wing in the sky. I never did learn to mend the other thing."

Sefa gave the order the way a Warden gives the hard ones — quietly, and without pretending it was anything else. "Kithrel. Take her in. Gentle — no irons, she's a frightened kid, not a brawler. But she's heard every word of ours, and we don't get to let that walk out the door." She turned her head to Brede, because she wouldn't do it behind her back. "Wing-Master. I'm sorry. She knows the whole of it, and the second she's loose she's a danger to the meet, to Esk, to *you* most of all — they'll know you turned the moment she reports. Taking her in is the only thing that keeps you alive long enough to matter. And you have my word, the one I gave you. No harm. She's handled as a scared girl who got caught in something. You'll know where she's held, and you'll be let to see her. I'll stand on that the same as I'd stand on a sword."

The old surgeon looked at her a long, terrible moment, then down at the heap of her apprentice, then closed her eyes and gave one small, broken nod. "...Aye. You're right and I hate that you're right." Fierce, through the tears: "Take her gentle, courier. And you — keep that word, Rocksquill, or I'll set your wing crooked after all."

Kithrel crouched by the girl. "Come on, Pell. Up you come. Nobody's going to hurt you." And the worst of it: Pell didn't fight. She let herself be drawn up by the elbow and turned toward the door, moving like something already gone, still wrapped around the pin, eyes fixed on nothing. At the threshold she didn't look back at Brede. That was the thing that broke the old woman — not the arrest. The not-looking-back.

"You carry a message better than I give one," Sefa told Kithrel at the stair-head. "Say it your way."

Something in the soot-streaked face eased — a courier handed her own reins. "Aye. I'll tell her: one parcel, fragile, handle soft; the nest's been kicked over so come before the birds scatter; and there's a grey hen here laying eggs worth the flight if the fox visits quiet." The ghost of her old grin. "She'll have it untangled before I've landed." A last look back. "Don't let the butcher finish you while I'm out. I've gone into one fire for you this week."

Then she was gone down the stair, the girl moving hollow at her side, out into the morning gales. The surgery settled into quiet — the gulls, the gauze breathing — and Brede fitted the loupe back over her eye, lifted the wax-paper swab off the wing-root, and went back to the slow, expert business of giving Sefa back the sky. "Hold still," she murmured, gentler than she'd been all morning, the grief gone down into her hands where she knew what to do with it. "This part I can mend."

---

A while passed — enough for Brede to close the wing, bind it in stiff waxed wrappings and a light splint-frame, and have Sefa shifted off the cradle onto a propped recovery cot where she could sit up and look a person in the eye. The wing was a dull throb under the binding now. She couldn't lift it. But it was hers again, and Brede said it would fly.

She heard Esk before she saw her, playing the cover loud enough for any ear on the stair: "—no, I'll see her myself, I don't care if she's sleeping, one of my Wardens burns a wing off saving half a tenement and I'll damn well sit at her bedside—" and then she was through the door, big and brisk in company-grey, and shut it with her heel and the volume went out of her like a snuffed lamp.

"...You look like something the Veyl reefs spat back," she said, and there was no joke under it. "Kithrel found me. Gave me your parcel — she's in a quiet room with two of my steadiest and a blanket and nobody's laid a finger on her, before you ask, because Kithrel made *very* sure I knew you'd promised it. We'll come back to that promise, you and I."

Then she took proper measure of the old Roak woman by the instrument table — the bloodied apron, the closed-hand pin, the red-rimmed eyes — and the two of them weighed each other a moment, the commander and the wing-master, two people used to being the most dangerous one in any room.

Esk spoke first. "Wing-Master Brede. My Warden says you're the door into the thing that's been growing under my city, and that you opened it of your own will at some cost. She also says there's a clock on us now. So I'll be plain and I'd be grateful if you'd answer plain. The closed hand. How many, how high, and how long before they know the floor's gone out from under them and scatter."

Brede glanced once at Sefa — *is this one safe* — and Sefa gave her the smallest nod. *He's the real one. Tell her.*

It came out flat and grief-worn, and it was worse and smaller than they'd feared, both at once. "The Stillhand's never been large. A few dozen, guild-wide, most of my generation. The *rot* is smaller still." Four names, laid down like instruments on a tray. "Aldous Penmarrow — senior man, respected, and that's the danger of him; he lends the young ones a grey head to hide behind. Yew Castellan, two-and-twenty and certain as only the young and the cruel get. Marrek. The Tasso woman. There'll be one or two more I don't know. I'll write you every name I'm sure of before you leave."

"And above them," Esk said. "You said *how high* like it scared you."

"Because it should scare us both. Vesh ran a feeding-cult under your windlane approach for a *season.* Provisioned it. Grew a scar. And no writ-hand ever came down on him, no inquiry, no guild discipline though there were whispers enough. That doesn't happen to a lone bad surgeon. Someone kept the Wardens off that gallery, and someone in my guild made sure the Pinion Council never *looked.*" She said the name like it tasted of rust. "Pinion-Master Halloway Crane. Head of the guild. I can't prove he wears the pin. But every door that should have opened on Vesh, Crane kept shut — and the writs that kept your people off that approach didn't come from any guild. They came from over all our heads."

Esk went very quiet at that. *Over all our heads* meant the Crown. The Spirarchy. The same hand that kept other lids screwed down in this city. Sefa watched her decide not to chase that thread out loud in a surgery, and file it somewhere deep and cold.

"The clock," Esk said instead.

"Pell tends a drop tonight — the third side-mouth, ninth-night provisions. When she doesn't come, and doesn't come home, they'll know by the small hours." Brede's voice caught on the girl's name. "And there's worse. With Vesh dead, the one holding the rot together now isn't Penmarrow or any of the loud ones. It's the keeper. Wren Sallowmend." She looked at Sefa. "Vesh's elder sister. Colder than him and twice as organised. Vesh was the *belief.* Wren is the *hands.* She was in that gallery and she walked out of it. She's the one you actually have to catch — and the one who'll smell this first."

Esk stood a moment, soot and lamplight, doing the commander's arithmetic. Then she turned to the cot. "Rocksquill. You can't fly and you can't fight, so you'll not be on tonight's wing, and don't argue it. But you've got the one thing I'm short of and can't make in an afternoon. You've got the only person who's been inside the rot and might still be reachable, sitting in a quiet room three streets over. Pell knows the names I don't. The drops, the faces, where Wren roosts." Her eyes held Sefa's, level and unsentimental. "And you swore that girl wouldn't be squeezed. So talk to me about your promise. Because in about six hours I have to take a keeper who burns rooms full of people, and the kid you vouched for is the fastest road to her. I'll keep your word if you tell me to. I'd just like you to look me in the eye when you tie my hands."

And Sefa felt the whole ugly war of it, in the space of Esk's waiting.

The rage came up first, and it came up strong — Wren Sallowmend's face she'd never seen clear through the smoke, the casks, the families on the mats, the fledge Brede couldn't save, a voice in her that sounded like the fire itself. *She'll do it again, to a hundred more, and you'll hobble the one wing that can catch her for a promise to a kid who spat in your face? Stop the quiet. Whatever it takes.* It was loud. It very nearly had her.

And then the other thing held. Quieter. Older. The thing that had made her a Warden instead of just a sharp pair of wings. *A Warden's word is the one thing the Stillhand can't counterfeit. The day you torture a frightened girl for the greater good, you haven't beaten the closed hand. You've joined it, in better colours.*

She looked Esk in the eye, the way she'd been asked, and tied her hands.

"Keep my word, Commander. No squeezing. Pell stays a scared kid in a warm room, untouched, exactly like I swore to the woman giving you four names she'll hang for. If we catch Wren by breaking faith with the first person who trusted us out of that fellowship, then the next one never comes out, and the one after that. The code's not a luxury we spend when it's expensive. It's the recruiting tool. Brede turned because a Warden was straight with her. Keep me straight." She let a breath go, and offered the road instead of the wall. "And we don't need her. Pell tends the third side-mouth drop tonight — and Pell's in your warm room. So the drop goes untended, and someone comes to find out why. You don't squeeze the girl, ser. You sit on her route and you let Wren walk to you."

Esk held her eye through all of it. Then, slowly, something settled in her face, and it wasn't disappointment. It was the look she'd given on the stair the night she wouldn't let Sefa carry the fire. "...Aye," she said quietly. "That's the right answer and the harder one, and I wanted to be sure you knew it was both before I let you give it." She straightened, and the commander came back into her voice. "The drop, then. Brede's names tonight, quiet and simultaneous. Eyes on Crane, no hands. And my best wing sitting on that third side-mouth in the dark, waiting for a keeper who thinks she's coming to scold an apprentice." A grim flicker. "She'll come herself. The organised ones always check the thing that went wrong with their own eyes."

She gave the wing-master a short nod that had a great deal in it, and went to the door, and put the cover back on like a coat. The volume came up. "—rest, that's an order, I'll have no heroics out of you for a fortnight—" and then she was gone, down into the morning and the long day of quiet arrangements that would end after dark in a bone-mouth that smelled of nothing.

Sefa lay back on the cot. She couldn't fly. She couldn't fight. She'd built the whole of the night that was coming and she'd not see a moment of it, and somewhere across the city a girl who should have been an ally was wrapped around a pin in a warm room, hating her.

Brede came and sat by her without being asked, an old surgeon with nothing left to mend for the moment, and the two of them watched the gauze breathe in the windows and the light go long and gold across the lime-washed bone, and neither of them said the third name out loud.

"You did a clean thing in a filthy week," Brede said at last.

"Doesn't feel clean."

"No," the Wing-Master said. "The clean ones never do."
