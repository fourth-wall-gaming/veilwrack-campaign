# Chapter Two — The Last Supper

They went down again that night, the second time, to watch — and split at the gallery mouth. Kithrel stayed there in the shadow with three flares and Esk's runner. Sefa went up the north scaffold into the vault of the supper-room on half-furled wings — dead air bites only half of nothing, and shoves the other half about the room like a held breath let go — and folded onto the high perch and became part of the bone.

The room woke beneath her. The keeper came first and lit the tallow down the half-circle of mats, and the chamber went amber, and the shadows climbed the vault toward Sefa. Then six families filed in and took the mats like mourners taking pews. Then the Hushed, led not driven, seven of them, the tallow-flames leaning and guttering as the dead air came in with them. Among them was a small woman with ink worn into the webbing of her fingers, her eyes open and moving and nothing behind them. Derra Tarrow.

Then three came through the felt arch. A big silent Ossuin who took post by the casks. A woman in grey with an ash-censer swinging in her hand. And between them, unhurried, a soft-faced Vael with clean hands and a small closed hand of bone at his collar. Vesh.

He lifted his empty hands to the mats and spoke in a warm, carrying voice.

"Friends. You've fed them faithfully, and they've grown. Tonight they're ready to give back what the world gave them — and you'll have your beloveds returned to the air they came from. All of you. Tonight is the last supper. After this, no one need pay again."

The big man laid his hand on an oil-cask.

Sefa's hatchet was already turning in her fingers. She threw it. It went out of the high dark and into the wrist on the cask, and the oil choked off, and every amber-lit face in the room tipped up toward a perch that had held nothing a heartbeat before.

Vesh had a spark-striker in his clean hand over the spilled oil. He did not crank it.

"A Warden. Of course." His thumb rested on the wheel. "Come down, feather. You don't want to be up there when this room goes to sleep. There's no flying in dead air. And there's no flying at all once I turn my wrist."

She rose to her height and rang the falchion out of its sheath for them all to see and threw her voice down. "You're surrounded, surgeon. The whole draft's in these galleries."

"No," he said, almost kind. "There are no other wings. I'd have heard them. This air carries a lie better than it carries a flock."

But the families had heard her name the casks. The grey-crested Lanhawk man came up off his mat. "Vesh. He said it was a blessing. He said our daughter would only—"

Vesh sent the big man to the arch and kept the striker, and Sefa stopped wasting breath on him.

"To the flares!" she shouted, the whole launch-deck of her lungs behind it. "Go! The wing's at the door!"

The families broke for the mouth. Kithrel's flare went up red and hung, and her voice came thin across the room. "Here! Eyes on the light — move!"

Sefa folded her wings and dropped. Nine metres, straight down, the falchion out front, the dive she was born for — and the dead air took it. Her wings found their half of nothing at the bottom and the dive came wrong and the falchion opened on empty, and Vesh simply leaned a hand's-width aside as she fell past his shoulder. She wrenched it back with the last thing she had, and the blade — the wide cutting edge Esk had put in her hand — came down through the root of his left wing and did not stop. The wing came away. He folded to the floor, and the pin winked once in the tallow-light.

His thumb finished its work on the way down.

The room took a breath of fire. It ran out from where he fell in two bright tongues, one along the swept path toward the mats and one curling back toward the casks, and the dead air did nothing to it but sit there and let it climb. Heat slapped her across the face. The Hushed pulled back from the light, rooted and shuddering.

She took up the dying man to carry him out — the face Esk wanted — and then the first cask began to sing, that rising kettle-shriek of oil going to vapour, and she knew it the way every Warden knows it. She rammed his body into the cask and threw herself the other way, for the girl. The cask burst. It picked her up and threw her and the fire caught in the feathers of her left wing.

She came down at Derra's feet, and the fire on her wing went small. In the dead hush that wrapped the silent girl the flames starved and ducked — and her own breath went thin and flat in her chest, the air giving nothing back.

She could not fly. She gathered the girl against her and held what breath she had and ran for the mouth. Fifteen metres of fire toward a red star. She made ten of them. Then the held breath turned to a fist in her chest and her legs stopped believing in the floor, and she went down five metres short, the dark closing in from every edge.

She did not take the rope for herself. She looped it around Derra and shoved the girl the last terrible distance toward Kithrel.

"Got her — I've got her!"

The hush went out of the room on the rope with the girl, and the fire remembered Sefa was there, and the dark won and took her down into it.

Kithrel dropped the line and came back into the fire for her and dragged her clear as a second cask blew.

She woke on the cold bone of the gallery floor, real air sawing into her lungs, the sweetest thing she had ever breathed. Kithrel had two fists in her harness.

"There she is. There she is, you stubborn squill. I went in for you. Cask went off behind us — you owe me a drink."

Sefa sat up and looked at her wing. The feathers were burned to black quills, the membrane stiff, the whole of it hanging wrong. She looked at it a long time. Then she looked at Kithrel.

"Fuck," she said.

"Yeah," Kithrel said. "That about covers it."

---

The fire had its own wind now — a hot, wrong updraught where there should have been none, sucking the smoke up the dead-air shaft she had climbed an hour ago. And the cold of the gallery-mouth stone under her was the only kind thing in the room. Her left wing would not answer. She did not look at it again. She already knew.

Down the throat of the under-stair, jammed into the dark where the smoke still thinned, the rescued were a knot of shapes. Six families. The girl among them, ink-fingered and still, her scorched wing held off her body like a broken fan, sitting the way the Hushed sit. Esk's rescue-line still looped under the girl's arms. The smoke was coming down the ceiling toward all of them in a slow grey tide.

"Can you carry me as far as the stair," Sefa said.

Kithrel didn't argue it. She got a shoulder under the good wing and levered her up off the stone, and the room tilted and came level, and the legs held. They were the one part of her the fire hadn't taken. The two of them made one limping animal, the ruined wing trailing, Kithrel's breath sawing in her ear.

She turned it into orders, because orders are what hold a frightened flock together. "On me. All of you — on the Warden. Up the stair, hands on the wall, don't run." They peeled off the dark and started to move, herding their own. The girl rose when the others rose, that terrible Hushed obedience, and let the rope lead her. It was working. They were moving, two metres at a time, ahead of the smoke.

And then the grey-crested man stopped.

He turned back — not up the stair, *back*, toward the felt arch and the orange roar behind it, toward the place a name he kept saying had gone into the fire. "She's still in there," he said, to no one, to the flames. "My — she's still—" and he took a step toward the heat, against the whole current of everyone else. Someone grabbed his sleeve and missed. Four metres from her. The fire four metres past him. Behind her the line wavered — one man breaking ranks can unmake the whole of it.

She had a half-second and two legs that wouldn't carry her to him in time. So she used the only thing that was faster than walking.

The curse came first, short and ugly and aimed past him at the thing in the fire — *you will not have him too* — and the command came on its heels, the Warden's voice, the one that does not ask. It came a heartbeat late. On any other night it would have nailed his feet to the floor the instant it left her. This night his daughter was in a fire and grief is louder than rank, and he flinched at it and half-turned and kept going anyway.

She found the word again. Threw it harder. *Harl.* His name and the order behind it, all the launch-deck she had left.

It landed.

He stopped, one stride short of the heat, the air peeling in front of his face. His shoulders came down. The fight went out of him all at once and he sagged, and the man beside him got a fist in his sleeve that held this time. "Come on, Harl. Come on. The Warden says up. We go up." Whatever had been in her voice, they had decided to believe it. The line knitted shut behind her.

"Nice," Kithrel breathed against her ear, hauling her on. "Do that again if anyone else gets clever."

---

So they went up. Slow, the smoke at their heels, the ruined wing dragging the stair behind her like a broken sail. A Warden and her partner making one animal, six families and a silent girl strung along the wall with their hands on the cold bone. The supper room fell away below into its own light. The stair bent, and bent again, and the air began to taste of *night* instead of oil —

— and that was when she heard them. Above, on the windlane approach: the double-beat of working wings, a lot of them, and a voice she knew carving down through the dark.

"*LANTERNFALL DRAFT — CALL OFF, who's down there!*" Esk's voice, and behind it the wink of a warden lantern swinging into the gallery throat, and the relief of a dozen wings finally answering Kithrel's flare.

Sefa tapped Kithrel's shoulder twice — the old courier signal, *your call* — and let her hand drop. Kithrel got it. She always got it.

"*Gallery mouth — Kithrel of the Moult!*" she shouted up the throat, and her voice had the carry Sefa's used to before tonight. "*Six families and one Hushed, all walking, all out! Cult leader's dead — burned with the room! Fire's below us and spreading — we need medics and we need the stair cleared! And send someone for the Rocksquill, she's down a wing and she's done!*"

A pause. Then Esk's voice came back flat and fast: "*Understood. Hold the mouth. Wing's coming to you.*" Lanternlight swung, and the first wardens dropped into the throat in a clatter of pinions — a medic among them, a stretcher-sling, two more peeling toward the families with water and steady hands. The grey-crested man let himself be led. The girl went unresisting into a warden's care, ink-fingers and all, and somewhere far back in Sefa's skull a thought filed itself for later — *her father, he needs to know she's out* — and not now. Now was for later.

Now she swore.

She picked a good one — long, heartfelt, anatomically ambitious, the kind her old wing-sergeant would have docked her pay for — and she folded down onto the cold stone of the stair with her back against the bone wall and her ruined wing trailing across the step, and she stopped holding herself up. Kithrel slid down next to her, shoulder to shoulder, both of them filthy and smoking faintly. Above, the draft did the work now. Below, the fire ate the place where a soft-voiced man had tried to put a whole congregation to sleep.

"You're buying the drinks," Kithrel said.

"I'm buying the drinks," Sefa agreed.
